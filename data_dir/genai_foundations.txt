GENERATIVE AI FOUNDATIONS: MODELS, DATA, AND TRAINING
====================================================

Generative AI refers to a family of machine learning systems that learn statistical patterns from data and then produce new content—text, images, audio, code, or structured outputs—that resembles what they learned. In teaching the topic, it helps to start with the core idea that these models do not “retrieve” answers in the same way a search engine does. Instead, they estimate the most likely continuation (or completion) of an input based on the distribution they learned during training, and they can sample from that distribution in different ways to trade off creativity, diversity, and determinism.

Most modern text-based generative systems are built on transformer architectures. A transformer processes sequences using self-attention, a mechanism that lets the model weigh relationships between tokens (subword units) regardless of distance in the input. This enables the model to capture long-range dependencies, such as how an early clause constrains later grammar or how a requirement in a spec should influence a later implementation detail. When teaching transformers, it is useful to emphasize what “attention” is and is not: it is a differentiable operation used during inference and training, not a humanlike focus or intention. The model’s behavior emerges from optimization, not from explicit reasoning rules.

Training typically begins with a large-scale pretraining stage. In pretraining, the model sees vast amounts of text and learns to predict missing tokens or the next token, adjusting millions or billions of parameters to reduce prediction error. This process builds general-purpose representations: syntax, semantics, factual associations, domain jargon, and common patterns of explanation. However, pretraining alone often yields outputs that are unhelpful, unsafe, or misaligned with user intent because the objective is generic likelihood, not usefulness. Therefore, many systems add alignment steps such as supervised fine-tuning on instruction data and preference-based optimization (for example, training the model to prefer responses that human raters judge as more helpful and safer).

Data is a principal determinant of model behavior, and instruction design benefits from making data concepts explicit. Teachers can introduce the idea of a “training distribution” and show how biases in sources, languages, and topics propagate into what the model can generate. Coverage affects performance: if a model rarely saw a niche format (such as a specific corporate template) it will struggle to reproduce it reliably. Quality affects robustness: noisy or contradictory data increases the probability of hallucination-like artifacts. Licensing and provenance matter for ethical and legal reasons, and these considerations become especially tangible when students create domain-specific datasets for fine-tuning.

Inference is the process of generating outputs from a trained model. A key teaching point is that output quality is not solely a function of “how smart” the model is; it is also controlled by decoding parameters and constraints. Temperature, top-p (nucleus sampling), and top-k sampling influence how much the model explores lower-probability tokens. Lower temperature and smaller top-p make outputs more deterministic and conservative; higher values increase variety but also the risk of incoherence. In structured tasks, constrained decoding—such as forcing JSON schema compliance, using tool calls, or applying grammar constraints—often improves reliability more than simply asking the model to “be precise.”

Because generative models are not databases, they can produce plausible but incorrect statements. This is best taught as a predictable failure mode of probabilistic generation rather than as a mysterious defect. When a model lacks grounding, it can interpolate patterns that sound credible. Educators can introduce grounding methods—retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), citations from trusted corpora, and tool-using agents—to connect generation to verifiable sources. A useful classroom exercise is to compare answers produced “from memory” with answers produced under a requirement to quote or cite retrieved evidence.

Finally, learners benefit from a mental model of capability limits. Generative AI can be excellent at drafting, summarizing, translating, and transforming information, and it can support reasoning-like behavior in constrained environments. Yet it remains sensitive to prompt phrasing, can reflect social biases, and may fail silently when uncertain. Teaching should therefore include the skill of uncertainty management: asking for assumptions, requesting alternatives, verifying with external sources, and using evaluation checklists. These habits turn generative AI from a novelty into a dependable component of professional workflows.
